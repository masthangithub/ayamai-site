Does the sound of a word carry part of its meaning, across unrelated languages?

Sphota is an AYAMAI project on sound and meaning. Indian grammarians described meaning as something that “bursts forth” from sound (sphoṭa), and the Vedas were preserved for centuries purely by recitation. That prompted a question we want to test with today’s audio analysis and machine learning: are there sound patterns tied to meaning that hold across languages with no shared history? We do not assume the answer is yes.

Where the known evidence starts. The five vowels plotted by their first two resonances (formants), with approximate adult averages. Experiments since Sapir (1929) find that people link /i/ with small things and open or back vowels with large ones, and Blasi et al. (2016) found /i/ over-represented in words for “small” across thousands of languages. These are statistical tendencies, not rules.

What formants are

The voice has two parts. The vocal folds buzz at a basic rate, the pitch (f0), producing a sound rich in harmonics. The throat, mouth and lips then act as a filter, letting some frequencies through strongly and damping others.

The peaks this filter creates are called formants. They are numbered from the lowest: F1, F2, F3. Moving the tongue, jaw and lips changes the shape of the tube, which moves the formants, and that is how we make different vowels.

Pitch and formants are independent. Say “ee” in a low voice and then a high voice: the pitch changes, the formants stay roughly where they are, and listeners still hear “ee”. That is why formants, not pitch, identify vowels across speakers.

One moment of a neutral vowel, like the “a” in “about”. Thin lines are harmonics of the voice; the curve is the filter shaped by the mouth. Its peaks are the formants, near 500, 1500 and 2500 Hz for an adult male voice.

How to read the vowel chart

F1: how open the mouth is

A closed vowel like /i/ in “see” has a low F1, about 300 Hz. An open vowel like /a/ in “father”, with the jaw dropped, has a high F1, about 750 Hz.

F2: tongue front or back

With the tongue pushed forward, as in /i/, F2 is high, above 2000 Hz. With it pulled back, as in /u/ in “food”, F2 is low, under 1000 Hz. Rounding the lips lowers it further.

Why the axes run “backwards”

By convention F1 increases downward and F2 decreases to the right. Drawn this way, the chart matches the mouth seen from the side: the top is tongue high, the left is tongue forward. The five vowels form the familiar vowel triangle.

What the marks show

The dashed shape is the space the five vowels span. The red /i/ is over-represented in words for “small” across many languages. The red ring marks open and back vowels, which people judge as “large” in experiments.

What this means for Sphota

If sound carries some meaning across languages, this is one place it should be visible: words with related meanings would lean toward the same region of the chart. The known size effect fits a physical story too. Small resonating cavities make high frequencies, and many animals signal small size with high sounds, so /i/ for “small” may come from that frequency code rather than from meaning as such. Separating these explanations is the point of the study. The values shown are approximate averages for adult men; women’s and children’s formants are higher, so real analyses normalise for each speaker. MFCCs, the features used in speech recognition, summarise the same filter shape more coarsely.

Where the question comes from

These traditions shaped the question. We treat them as inspiration, not as evidence.

Bhartṛhari’s sphoṭa

In the Vākyapadīya (about the 5th century CE), meaning is grasped as a whole that is revealed through sound, rather than assembled letter by letter.

Vedic recitation

The Vedas were passed on orally with exacting rules for pronunciation (śikṣā), one of the world’s earliest systematic phonetics traditions.

Plato’s Cratylus

A dialogue on whether names belong to things by nature or by convention, the same debate modern linguistics calls iconicity versus arbitrariness.

What research already shows

Most of the link between sound and meaning is arbitrary: “water”, “jal” and “mizu” share little. But careful studies find small, repeatable departures from pure arbitrariness. Our work starts from these findings, and from the explanations that already account for them.

  • Shape and size from sound

    People across cultures match “bouba” to round shapes and “kiki” to spiky ones, and “mal” to a larger table than “mil”.

    Köhler 1929; Sapir 1929; Ramachandran and Hubbard 2001
  • Patterns across thousands of languages

    Some basic meanings show sound preferences beyond chance, such as /i/ in words for “small” and nasal sounds in words for “nose”.

    Blasi et al. 2016, PNAS
  • The frequency code in animals

    Across many species, low and harsh calls signal size or threat, and high, tonal calls signal fear or appeasement.

    Morton 1977; Ohala 1984
  • A known alternative explanation

    Words like “mama” and “papa” are similar worldwide because they are among the first sounds infants can make, which adults then assign to parents.

    Jakobson 1960

Our question, stated so it can fail

From recordings alone, can a model predict which basic meaning a word expresses, in language families it never saw during training, better than a model that knows only which phonemes the word contains?

Testing on unseen language families rules out shared ancestry and borrowed words. Comparing against a phoneme-only model asks whether the acoustic detail adds anything beyond the categories linguists already use. A clear “no” is a useful result too, and we will publish it.

Method

Built from existing, well-tested tools. We are not inventing new models for the first round.

  1. GatherRecordings of the same basic meanings, such as a Swadesh list, in many languages.
  2. SegmentForced alignment cuts each recording into words and individual sounds.
  3. MeasurePer sound: formants F1 to F3, pitch, MFCCs and mel spectrograms.
  4. PredictSimple classifiers learn meaning from sound, alongside a phoneme-only baseline.
  5. Hold outScore only on language families left out of training.

Tools: Librosa, Praat through Parselmouth, Montreal Forced Aligner, scikit-learn and PyTorch. Data sources we are looking at: PHOIBLE phoneme inventories, AI4Bharat’s Kathbath, and the Vāksañcayaḥ Sanskrit speech corpus from IIT Bombay.

Questions we ask of every result

The study builds its own skepticism in. Any pattern we find has to survive these before we report it as more than a coincidence.

Is it only mouth shape?

Rounded lips lower every formant. A shared resonance may be a fact about anatomy, not meaning.

Is it infant babbling?

Kinship words may follow the order in which babies learn sounds, as Jakobson proposed.

Is it history?

Related or neighbouring languages share words. Results must hold across unrelated families and isolates.

Did we go looking for it?

Meanings, features and tests are fixed before analysis, so we cannot pick only the examples that fit.

A research assistant built from agents

Alongside the experiments, we are designing an agent loop to keep the literature review honest and current. Each role is a separate step in a LangGraph workflow, and the Critic can send the loop back for more evidence.

ResearcherSearches papers and datasets on phonetics, sound symbolism and bioacoustics.
LibrarianRetrieves from our own notes and texts on Indian and Greek theories of language.
AnalystRuns the audio feature scripts and reports what the measurements show.
CriticNames the weakest link, offers a counter-explanation, and sets the next question.

If something holds up

Possible uses, all conditional on the results.

Low-resource languages

Acoustic features that transfer across languages could help speech tools for languages with little recorded data.

Voice and wellbeing

The same feature pipeline applies to vocal biomarkers, where speech is studied as a signal of health. We have an early filter-bank prototype tested on synthetic data.
